# Growing a volunteer team

The gap between people who said yes and people standing in the room on Saturday is the whole job. Recruitment gets attention; retention is what actually decides the size of your team in month three.

## Recruit for a specific thing

"Get involved" converts badly because it asks the person to do the work of imagining a role. A specific ask converts because it is answerable:

- "Can you knock doors for three hours on Saturday morning?"
- "Can you spend an hour on Tuesday night answering texts?"

Both are easy to say no to, which is the point. A clear no now is worth more than a vague yes that evaporates.

## Two doors in, both of them managed

- **Invitations** – you bring someone in directly and set their role from the start. Use this for people you know.
- **Join requests** – people ask to join and you approve them. Use this for public sign-up links, so an open door is not an unguarded one.

Keep the public sign-up link genuinely public – on the site, in the email footer, at the bottom of texts. Most people who volunteer were not asked personally; they found a link at the moment they felt like acting.

## The first shift decides everything

Whether someone comes back is mostly decided in their first two hours. Protect that:

- **Pair a first-timer with someone experienced.** Never send a new volunteer out alone, however confident they seem.
- **Give them the checklist.** The getting-started checklist walks a new member through their first steps rather than leaving them on a dashboard wondering what to do.
- **Make the first shift short.** Two hours, not five.
- **Debrief them personally.** Five minutes at the end, by name. Ask what surprised them.

A first-timer who has a good first shift becomes a regular. One who is dropped on a hard turf alone does not come back, and you will never find out why.

## Give people a role, not a task list

Roles are how people know they belong. Uprise gives you role-based access so an organiser, a phone-bank lead and a data volunteer each see what they need and nothing they shouldn't – but the point is as much social as technical. "I'm the turf-cutter" is an identity; "I sometimes help out" is not.

Promote early and visibly. The volunteer who has done four shifts should be running the briefing at their sixth.

## Make the calendar the single source of truth

People show up when they know when and where to be. Put events, shifts and ad-hoc entries in one calendar so a volunteer sees the whole picture rather than assembling it from three group chats.

For public things, use the RSVP page so supporters sign themselves up instead of an organiser fielding every reply by hand. Export the list when you need it for door codes, catering or follow-up.

## Shifts, not intentions

A named shift is a kept commitment far more often than a good intention. Slot people into defined shifts and confirm them individually the day before – one message, to one person, asking them to reply. Group confirmations confirm nothing.

## Keep the team feeling like a team

The live action room is the difference between a scattered set of individuals and a group who can feel each other working. Volunteers see the operation moving; leads can reach everyone at once with broadcast push when the last hour matters.

Small things compound here. Use people's names. Say what the numbers were at the end of the day. Tell them what changed because of the doors they knocked.

## The second Saturday problem

Enthusiasm on day one was never the hard part. The fifth Saturday, in the rain, is. What holds people is unglamorous and mostly non-digital: a clear role, a calendar they can read, a shift with their name on it, someone who notices when they arrive, and evidence that the work mattered.

Build those and sign-ups turn into a team. Skip them and you will re-recruit the same number of people every month forever.
